Solo-authored

Advertising versus Screening?
Online-Collected Data in Lending Markets

Main result: Online consumer data expands credit access through better screening but also lets lenders monetize search frictions via targeted advertising, raising rates by 14 bp for likely online shoppers.

Presentations: 39th Australasian Finance and Banking Conference (2026, scheduled), AFA PhD Student Poster Session (2027, scheduled)

PaperAbstract

As mortgage shopping moves online, lenders gain data on borrowers’ search and browsing activity. These data improve screening, lower rates, and expand credit. But they also support targeted advertising that reduces comparison shopping and raises markups. Which force dominates determines who benefits from online data.

I exploit the staggered adoption of state privacy laws limiting these uses, comparing borrowers at the same lender across states. Restricting data lowers mortgage rates by 14 basis points at advertising-reliant lenders: lower markups outweigh weaker screening. Consistent with weaker screening, approval rates fall one percentage point for credit scores below 750 and rise half a point above it.

Consistent with weaker targeting, paid-search clicks fall 7% at lenders using advertising-platform data. A search model shows that privacy shifts surplus from lenders to borrowers but reduces total surplus because search becomes costlier and screening weaker. In digital lending, data-use rules shape search and credit access.

Working Papers

Collateral and Technology Adoption

with E. Benmelech and S. Rotberg

Paper (coming soon)Abstract

The technologies that transform economies – steam, diesel, electricity, computing – arrive embodied in equipment and are often financed with debt secured by that equipment. We model adoption with endogenous collateral values: the resale value of new machines rises with the number of firms operating them and the standardization of their designs, while each departing user drains the pool of buyers for the old technology. Secured credit generates strategic complementarity in adoption: each switch improves the financing terms of prospective adopters and worsens those of firms left behind. Finance, not technology alone, governs the pace of diffusion. Standardization amplifies the complementarity: fewer designs deepen the resale market behind every machine, so a mandate to standardize also loosens credit. The dieselization of American railroads illustrates the mechanism: diesels purchased with equipment trust certificates displaced steam, adoption surged after wartime standardization, and the steam resale market collapsed.

Downside-Risk Sharing

with L. Rossi

Revise and Resubmit at the European Economic Review

Presentations: Midwest Macroeconomics Spring Meeting (2025)

PaperAbstract

Risk sharing is typically estimated using models that rely on both tails of the income growth distribution, yet agents care more about downside risks. We focus on negative income shocks, developing a methodology to estimate channels of downside-risk sharing and extend it to increasingly severe shocks – an issue important to policymakers but overlooked in prior work. We find that federal transfers and taxes absorb a large share of income-growth fluctuations, a result earlier studies understated. Overall, our findings challenge policy arguments based on past research portraying public risk sharing as a relatively weak channel.

Subnational Income, Growth, and the COVID-19 Pandemic

with M. A. Choudhary, I. Dal Barco, I. A. Haqqani, and N. Limodio

The World Bank Economic Review, 39(2), 362–376, 2025

PaperAbstract

Using real-time data and machine-learning methods, we produce monthly aggregates on gross national income (GNI) for 147 Pakistani districts between 2012 and 2021. We use them to understand whether and how the COVID-19 pandemic affected Pakistan's growth and subnational income distribution. Three findings emerge from our analysis. First, districts experienced a sizable decline in income during the pandemic, as their monthly growth rate dropped on average by 0.133 percentage points. Second, a larger income drop occurred in districts with a higher COVID-19 incidence, corresponding to urban areas characterized by a higher population density. Third, COVID-19 caused a decline in income inequality across districts, with more affluent districts experiencing more negative income growth during the pandemic.

What Reduces Frictions in Trade Finance? The Role of Banks, Relationships, and Policy

with N. B. Adou and J.-W. Chien

PEDL Research Note, CEPR, 2025

Policy NoteAbstract

The African continent is often envisioned as the future industrial hub of the planet. However, it accounts for no more than 3% of total global trade. A key factor behind this performance is the limited access to financial services. Understanding how trade finance works in emerging markets is undoubtedly the first step for developing future interventions. The present work surveys importers and exporters in Kenya to understand their demand for different financial products and intermediaries. It also sheds light on the role of relationship banking and Pan-African banks in reducing friction in trade financing. Lastly, it looks at the awareness of recent trade agreements affecting the cost of trade and payment services.
